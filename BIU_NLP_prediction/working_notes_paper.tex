\begin{abstract}
Relation extraction (RE) --- the automatic identification of semantic
relationships between entities in text --- is a core task in information
extraction and knowledge base construction. The recent emergence of large
language models (LLMs) has opened new possibilities for RE without task-specific
fine-tuning, through zero- and few-shot prompting. This capability is
particularly valuable for \emph{multilingual historical} settings, where
annotated data is scarce, text is noisy from OCR digitization, and archaic
language poses barriers to standard NLP pipelines. HIPE 2026 is a shared task
at CLEF 2026 that targets exactly this scenario: given a historical newspaper
article in German, English, or French, systems must classify each annotated
person--place pair along two relations --- \texttt{at} (was the person at the
location?) and \texttt{isAt} (is the location the person's current habitual
place?) --- evaluated by global macro-recall over 638 test pairs across three
languages plus a surprise out-of-domain French set. We describe the BIU NLP
system, which applies zero/few-shot prompting with chain-of-thought reasoning
and structured JSON output across four open-weight LLMs (Gemma-4-26B,
Gemma-3-27B, Mistral-Small-24B, Aya-Expanse-32B). Without any fine-tuning, our
best submission (Gemma-3-27B, zero-shot, English prompt) ranks \textbf{9th out
of 46} for French and \textbf{20th overall}; Gemma-4-26B ranks \textbf{3rd out
of 44} on the surprise binary evaluation. These results establish zero-shot LLM
prompting as a strong, accessible baseline for multilingual historical RE, and
we release all code, prompts, and results to encourage further exploration of
this underserved setting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Relation extraction (RE) is the task of automatically identifying and
classifying semantic relationships between entities in unstructured text. It is
a fundamental building block of information extraction, enabling the
construction of knowledge graphs, population of structured databases, and
downstream tasks such as question answering and fact verification. Traditional
RE systems required large annotated datasets and were trained in a fully
supervised setting, limiting their applicability to domains and languages where
such resources exist. The advent of large language models has transformed this
landscape: models with billions of parameters, pre-trained on vast multilingual
corpora, can now perform competitive RE through in-context learning --- providing
task instructions and a handful of examples at inference time, without updating
any model weights.

This shift is especially consequential for \textbf{historical and multilingual}
RE, where supervised data is almost never available at scale. Historical
newspaper archives represent a rich source of information about persons,
events, and geography across centuries, but their digitization introduces OCR
errors, archaic spelling, and unusual typography that undermine standard NLP
preprocessing. Furthermore, targeting three distinct languages simultaneously
--- German, English, and French, each with its own spelling conventions and
linguistic properties during the 19th and early 20th centuries --- imposes
requirements for genuine multilingual competence rather than mere code-switching.

The HIPE 2026 shared task at CLEF 2026 frames this challenge concretely. Given
a historical newspaper article from the Impresso digitized newspaper corpus, with
named entities (persons and places) already identified and linked to Wikidata,
the task is to qualify each person--place pair along two relations: \texttt{at}
(does the article provide evidence that the person was at the location at any
point?) and \texttt{isAt} (does the article suggest the location is the
person's current or habitual place at the time of publication?). The task was
designed to support research into historical mobility and life trajectories, and
is evaluated using global macro-recall --- a metric that requires systems to
recall non-\texttt{FALSE} instances rather than simply defaulting to the dominant
negative class. With 17 teams and 46--47 submissions per language, the task
provides a competitive setting for benchmarking approaches to this problem.

Our contribution is the BIU NLP system, developed at Bar-Ilan University. We
treat the task as a zero/few-shot text classification problem and evaluate four
open-weight instruction-tuned LLMs --- Gemma-4-26B (a Mixture-of-Experts
model), Gemma-3-27B, Mistral-Small-24B, and Aya-Expanse-32B --- under a
controlled experimental grid spanning prompt languages (English vs.\ native
document language) and few-shot counts (0, 3, 5, 10 in-context examples). Our
prompts instruct the model to act as a historian, provide explicit relation
definitions and logical constraints, and require chain-of-thought explanations
before each label, followed by a structured JSON output. No task-specific
fine-tuning is performed at any stage.

Our best submission (Gemma-3-27B, zero-shot, English prompt) achieves a global
macro-recall of 0.6529 on the French impresso test set, ranking \textbf{9th out
of 46} --- the highest rank among our runs. Overall, Gemma-3 ranks \textbf{20th
out of 46} submissions. In the out-of-domain generalization setting (surprise
French test set from a literary works corpus), Gemma-4-26B ranks \textbf{3rd
out of 44} in binary evaluation with a score of 0.8804, demonstrating strong
generalization despite underperforming on the in-domain impresso test. These
results show that careful prompt engineering with capable open-weight LLMs
constitutes a viable and accessible baseline for historical multilingual RE,
even without any fine-tuning.

This paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related
work on relation extraction, LLM-based approaches, and the challenges of
historical multilingual NLP. Section~\ref{sec:task} describes the task and
dataset in detail. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents our methodology. Section~%
\ref{sec:results} reports official results and development experiments.
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} analyzes key findings. Section~\ref{sec:future}
outlines directions for future work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Relation Extraction}

Relation extraction has been studied extensively in NLP over the past two
decades. Early systems relied on pattern-based rules, dependency tree kernels,
and feature-engineered classifiers, operating primarily on sentence-level
entity pairs with a small closed set of relation types. Neural approaches ---
including convolutional and recurrent networks applied to entity-contextualized
representations --- improved over these baselines on benchmark datasets such as
ACE-2004, ACE-2005, and TACRED. The introduction of pre-trained transformer
models (BERT and its variants) marked a major step forward, enabling
fine-tuned systems to achieve near-human performance on standard English
benchmarks.

Document-level RE, which requires aggregating evidence across multiple sentences
or paragraphs to determine whether two entities are related, has attracted
increasing attention through datasets such as DocRED and CDR. Document-level
RE is the more natural setting for historical newspaper text, where a person and
a place may be mentioned in different parts of a long article, and the
relationship must be inferred from the discourse structure rather than a single
syntactic construction.

Cross-lingual RE has been explored through multilingual pre-trained models
(mBERT, XLM-R) and through translation-based pipelines that map source text
into a pivot language before applying a monolingual RE system. Both approaches
require either parallel data or target-language annotated examples, which are
rarely available for historical domains.

\subsection{LLM Approaches to Relation Extraction}

Large language models have demonstrated strong in-context learning abilities
across a wide range of NLP tasks, including RE. Zero-shot prompting --- simply
describing the task and asking for a prediction --- has been shown to achieve
competitive results on standard RE benchmarks, particularly when the model is
given explicit relation definitions and label constraints. Few-shot prompting,
which provides labeled examples in the prompt, further improves performance,
especially when examples are selected to cover the label space and be
semantically similar to the target instance.

Structured output formats, such as JSON, have proven effective for extracting
relation triples from LLMs, since they decouple the relation classification
from the surface form of the output and facilitate parsing. Chain-of-thought
(CoT) prompting --- eliciting intermediate reasoning steps before the final
answer --- has been shown to improve performance on tasks that require
multi-step inference, such as identifying implicit or indirect evidence in text.
This is particularly relevant for relation qualification tasks where the
relationship between two entities is not explicitly stated but must be inferred
from context.

The application of LLMs to RE has been studied in general-domain settings, but
work on historical and low-resource settings remains limited. Historical text
presents additional challenges that are not encountered in contemporary NLP:
OCR noise that corrupts individual tokens, archaic vocabulary that may be
underrepresented in model pre-training data, and highly variable orthography
especially in older German and French sources.

\subsection{Challenges in Historical Multilingual Relation Extraction}

Historical newspaper RE sits at the intersection of several difficult
sub-problems. First, the text is not clean modern prose but digitized microfilm
output, which introduces systematic OCR errors: transpositions, substituted
characters (e.g., `\texttt{rn}' for `\texttt{m}'), hyphenation artifacts at
line breaks, and missing spaces. These errors can break tokenization, corrupt
entity mentions, and confuse models trained on clean text.

Second, the language of 19th and early 20th century newspapers differs
substantially from contemporary usage. German in particular underwent spelling
reforms, and French newspaper conventions of the period include forms and
abbreviations absent from modern corpora. Models pretrained primarily on
contemporary web text may have limited exposure to these historical registers,
even if they support the language in principle.

Third, the multilingual dimension of HIPE 2026 requires a single system
approach to handle German, English, and French simultaneously. These three
languages differ not only lexically but also in their morphological complexity
(German's case and gender inflection), their word order conventions, and their
patterns of entity mention. A system must generalize its relation qualification
reasoning across all three without language-specific adaptation.

Finally, the relation qualification task itself is semantically subtle. The
distinction between \texttt{at} and \texttt{isAt}, and between
\texttt{TRUE} and \texttt{PROBABLE} for \texttt{at}, requires careful
reading of temporal and evidential cues in the article, not just entity
co-occurrence detection. Most person--place pairs in any given article have no
meaningful spatial relation (\texttt{FALSE}), creating a strong class imbalance
that naive systems exploit by predicting the majority class.

\section{Task and Data}
\label{sec:task}

\subsection{Task Definition}

The HIPE 2026 relation qualification task is defined as follows. Given a
historical newspaper article with pre-annotated named entities (persons and
locations, linked to Wikidata where possible), and a set of candidate
\textbf{person--place pairs} extracted from the article, the system must assign
labels for two relations for each pair:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{at}}: Does the article provide textual evidence that
        the person was at the location at any point before the publication date?\\
        Labels: \texttt{TRUE}, \texttt{PROBABLE}, \texttt{FALSE}.

        \texttt{TRUE} applies when the article explicitly states or clearly
        implies the presence. \texttt{PROBABLE} applies when the article
        suggests a connection but the evidence is indirect or hedged.
        \texttt{FALSE} applies when no such evidence exists.

  \item \textbf{\texttt{isAt}}: Does the article support that the location
        is the person's current, ongoing, or habitual location at the time of
        publication?\\
        Labels: \texttt{TRUE}, \texttt{FALSE}.

        \texttt{isAt} is a stronger claim than \texttt{at}: it requires evidence
        of a present or habitual connection, not merely a past visit.
        It is never labelled \texttt{PROBABLE}.
\end{itemize}

The two relations are logically constrained: \texttt{isAt}~=~\texttt{TRUE}
implies \texttt{at}~=~\texttt{TRUE}; and \texttt{at}~=~\texttt{FALSE}
implies \texttt{isAt}~=~\texttt{FALSE}. These constraints reflect the
conceptual hierarchy: a current habitual location is necessarily a location
the person is at.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}

The primary metric is \textbf{global macro-recall} over both relations jointly.
Macro-recall is the unweighted average of per-class recall across all label
classes and both relations, giving equal weight to rare classes
(\texttt{TRUE}, \texttt{PROBABLE}, \texttt{isAt-TRUE}) and the frequent
\texttt{FALSE} class.

A system predicting all pairs as \texttt{FALSE} achieves approximately
\textbf{0.4167} macro-recall --- the dominant-class baseline. Any useful system
must substantially exceed this value by correctly recalling the minority
relation-positive cases. A secondary \textbf{binary evaluation} maps
\texttt{PROBABLE} predictions for \texttt{at} to \texttt{TRUE}, reducing the
problem to binary classification; this removes the partial-credit
\texttt{PROBABLE} class and provides an upper-bound view of recall on positive
instances.

\subsection{Dataset}

The data was provided by the Impresso project
(\url{https://impresso-project.ch}), which digitized and semantically enriched
historical European newspaper archives. The HIPE 2026 corpus extends the
HIPE-2022 v2.1 named entity-annotated collection, covering newspapers in
German, English, and French from roughly the 1780s to the 1950s. Named entity
annotations include surface mention offsets, entity type (person, location),
and Wikidata QID links where available. Person--place pairs were first
pre-annotated by an LLM ensemble, then manually reviewed and corrected by
domain experts.

\subsection{Label Distribution and Class Imbalance}

The label distribution in both training and development data is strongly skewed
toward \texttt{FALSE}. In practice, most person--place pairs within a given
newspaper article have no meaningful spatial relation: a political article may
mention dozens of persons and places in passing, but only a few involve genuine
at-relations or habitual locations. \texttt{TRUE} and \texttt{PROBABLE} for
\texttt{at} are relatively rare; \texttt{isAt}~=~\texttt{TRUE} is rarest of
all, since it requires evidence of a current or habitual connection, not merely
a co-mention.

This imbalance makes the task challenging for both discriminative and generative
systems. A model that defaults to \texttt{FALSE} achieves a non-trivial
macro-recall of 0.4167, and improving substantially beyond this baseline
requires correctly identifying minority-class instances.

\subsection{Data Characteristics}

The HIPE 2026 corpus presents several characteristics that make it distinct from
contemporary RE benchmarks:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{OCR noise}: Digitization from microfilm introduces character
        substitutions (e.g., `\texttt{rn}'~$\to$~`\texttt{m}'), missing or
        extra spaces, hyphenation artifacts at line boundaries, and corrupted
        proper nouns. Entity mentions in particular may appear with OCR errors
        that cause surface-form mismatches.

  \item \textbf{Archaic language}: 19th and early 20th century newspaper text
        uses vocabulary, spelling, and grammatical constructions that differ from
        contemporary usage. German sources in particular predate the 1901
        orthographic reform, and French sources use period-specific typography
        and abbreviation conventions.

  \item \textbf{Long documents with many entity pairs}: A single article may
        contain dozens of person--place pairs to classify. Models must correctly
        scope each prediction to the specific pair under consideration, without
        confusing evidence for one pair with evidence for another.

  \item \textbf{Implicit and indirect evidence}: Many positive instances
        (\texttt{TRUE} or \texttt{PROBABLE}) require inference from indirect
        cues --- for example, a person's role or title implying a location, or
        a reported action that entails presence in a place.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Our Approach}

We frame person--place relation qualification as a \textbf{per-pair text
classification} problem addressed through zero/few-shot prompting of large
language models, with no task-specific fine-tuning. Each person--place pair is
classified independently: the model receives the full article text and the two
entity mention strings (person and place), and produces a structured prediction
consisting of an explanation and a label for each of the two relations.

The motivation for this approach is threefold. First, the HIPE 2026 training
set, while informative, is small relative to the complexity of the task and the
three languages covered; fine-tuning on it risks overfitting, especially for
German and English, where fewer than 100 articles are available. Second,
modern instruction-tuned LLMs have been pre-trained on vast multilingual
corpora and have been shown to capture fine-grained semantic distinctions
relevant to historical text, even without domain-specific adaptation. Third,
zero/few-shot prompting allows rapid iteration and transparent debugging via the
chain-of-thought explanations the model produces.

\subsection{Prompt Design}

We developed structured prompts in both English and in the native document
language (German or French). Each prompt consists of six components:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{System role}: The model is instructed to act as a historian
        working on person--location relations in multilingual historical European
        newspapers, grounding its reasoning strictly in the article text.

  \item \textbf{Relation definitions}: Explicit definitions of \texttt{at} and
        \texttt{isAt}, including their temporal scope, valid label sets, and the
        distinction between \texttt{TRUE} and \texttt{PROBABLE} for \texttt{at}.

  \item \textbf{Decision guidance}: Labeling rules specifying when each label
        applies (e.g., \texttt{PROBABLE} only when evidence is indirect or
        hedged; \texttt{isAt} is never \texttt{PROBABLE}; logical constraints
        between the two relations must be respected).

  \item \textbf{Robustness rules}: Explicit instructions to handle OCR
        noise, line-break hyphenation artifacts, and historical spelling
        variation without relying on external knowledge or resolving ambiguities
        beyond what the article provides.

  \item \textbf{Chain-of-thought format}: Before assigning each label, the
        model is asked to produce a brief explanation (at most 100 words,
        citing only article evidence) for \texttt{at} and \texttt{isAt}
        separately. This grounds the decision in the text and provides
        interpretable traces.

  \item \textbf{Structured JSON output}: The final answer is a JSON object
        with keys \texttt{at\_explanation}, \texttt{at},
        \texttt{isAt\_explanation}, \texttt{isAt}. Requiring JSON encourages
        consistent, parseable output and separates the reasoning trace from the
        label.
\end{enumerate}

We hypothesized that native-language prompts might benefit models with weaker
historical German performance, while English prompts might generalize better
across all three languages for models with strong English instruction-following.

\subsection{Few-Shot Examples}

We experimented with 0, 3, 5, and 10 in-context examples per prompt. Examples
were drawn from the training set and selected to cover a mix of \texttt{TRUE},
\texttt{PROBABLE}, and \texttt{FALSE} label combinations for both relations,
avoiding a bias toward the majority \texttt{FALSE} class. Each example includes
the full article snippet, the person and place mentions, and the complete JSON
output with explanations.

At 10-shot, prompts reach several thousand tokens. We verified that all models
supported the required context lengths. For the final submission, 3-shot and
5-shot configurations were used for Gemma-4 (where few-shot improved
performance), while zero-shot was used for Gemma-3 and Mistral (where few-shot
did not consistently improve or sometimes degraded performance).

\subsection{Effect of Shot Count}

Increasing in-context examples consistently improved Gemma-4 up to 10 shots
(e.g., $+$0.12 macro-recall for German from 0-shot to 10-shot). For Gemma-3
and Mistral, few-shot examples were inconsistent and sometimes degraded
performance, possibly due to prompt length effects or reduced attention to the
target article as context grows longer. Accordingly, we submitted zero-shot
configurations for Gemma-3 and Mistral, and 3-shot or 5-shot for Gemma-4.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Gemma-3 as the Most Reliable System}

Gemma-3 (run2) is our strongest overall submission, ranking 20/46 and achieving
the best individual result among our runs (French, rank~9/46 main). Its
consistent zero-shot English performance across all three languages, without any
native-language adaptation, makes it a strong and convenient baseline for
multilingual historical RE. The particularly strong French result (0.6529) may
partly reflect the larger French training set (317 articles, 4,450 pairs),
which provides richer in-domain calibration data for example selection and
development-time evaluation.

\subsection{The Gemma-4 Anomaly}

The most striking finding is the divergence between Gemma-4's development and
test performance. On the dev set it was the clear winner (0.75--0.78
macro-recall across all three languages), yet on the impresso test sets it
ranked last among our runs (41/46 overall, with individual language ranks of
37/46, 36/47, and 42/46). Conversely, on the surprise French test it ranked
6/46 (main) and 3/44 (binary) --- the best among all three of our submissions.

Several hypotheses may explain this pattern:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Dev/test domain shift within impresso}: the dev and test splits
        may differ in newspaper titles, time periods, or topics, and Gemma-4 may
        have overfit to dev-set idiosyncrasies during configuration selection.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{PROBABLE} label calibration}: Gemma-4 may predict
        \texttt{PROBABLE} more frequently than other models. In the main
        evaluation this can hurt if the test distribution has fewer ambiguous
        cases. The binary evaluation, which maps \texttt{PROBABLE}~$\to$%
        ~\texttt{TRUE}, shows a milder score for Gemma-4 (0.52 vs.\ 0.44),
        consistent with over-prediction of \texttt{PROBABLE}.
  \item \textbf{Few-shot example bias}: the 3/5-shot examples selected from
        training data may not generalize to the impresso test distribution.
  \item \textbf{Surprise set affinity}: the literary works corpus may be
        stylistically closer to Gemma-4's pre-training distribution, explaining
        its strong out-of-domain generalization despite weaker in-domain
        performance.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Prompt Language Effects}

Native-language prompting helped Mistral most on German (0.5917 native
vs.\ 0.5389 English, 0-shot), consistent with Mistral Small's training
prioritizing modern European languages but being less robust on historical
German. For French, native prompts did not help any model meaningfully, likely
because English and French are both well-represented in all model pretraining
corpora. For Gemma-3, native prompts provided a modest benefit on German
(0.5622 native vs.\ 0.5299 English).

\subsection{Few-Shot Scaling}

Few-shot examples improved Gemma-4 consistently up to 10 shots but were
inconsistent for Gemma-3 and Mistral. This may reflect differences in
instruction-following capacity: Gemma-4's MoE architecture activates more
specialized parameters per token, enabling it to better integrate long in-context
demonstrations. Smaller or denser models may lose focus on the target pair as
prompt length grows. Retrieval-augmented example selection --- choosing examples
semantically similar to the target article --- could address this by providing
more relevant demonstrations at lower prompt lengths.

\subsection{Progress Beyond the All-FALSE Baseline}

All submitted runs exceed the all-FALSE baseline ($\approx$0.4167). Run2 achieves
0.5781 overall (main) and 0.6721 (binary), gains of $+$0.16 and $+$0.26
respectively. The French result (0.6529, rank~9/46) represents a gain of
$+$0.24, and the surprise-FR binary result (0.8804, rank~3/44) a gain of
$+$0.46. These gains demonstrate that structured LLM prompting with chain-of-thought
reasoning is a strong approach for this task, even without any task-specific
fine-tuning or domain adaptation.

\section{Perspectives for Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{Fine-tuned smaller models.}
The strong zero-shot performance of 24--27B parameter models motivates exploring
whether a smaller model (7B--13B) fine-tuned on the HIPE 2026 training set could
match this performance at significantly lower inference cost, and whether
continued pre-training on historical newspaper text further improves recall of
minority classes.

\textbf{Retrieval-augmented few-shot selection.}
Rather than fixed, randomly sampled in-context examples, selecting examples by
semantic similarity to the target pair (article topic, entity type, time period,
or newspaper) could improve few-shot performance across all models, especially
for the smaller models where fixed few-shot examples were inconsistent.

\textbf{Constrained decoding and post-processing.}
The logical constraints between \texttt{at} and \texttt{isAt}
could be enforced via constrained decoding during generation or via
post-processing rules applied to the model's JSON output, rather than relying
on the model to satisfy them through the prompt alone. This would eliminate
a source of prediction errors observed in our outputs.

\textbf{Cross-lingual transfer.}
The French training set is roughly $6\times$ larger than German and $9\times$
larger than English. Systematic cross-lingual transfer experiments --- training
on French and evaluating on German and English --- could address the data
imbalance and improve low-resource language performance, and would illuminate
how much of the French advantage reflects data quantity versus language-specific
model capacity.

\textbf{Analysis of the Gemma-4 gap.}
A detailed analysis comparing Gemma-4 predictions on dev versus test ---
examining label distributions, entity types, and article domains --- would
clarify whether the dev/test performance discrepancy is driven by
\texttt{PROBABLE} calibration, domain mismatch between splits, or few-shot
example selection artifacts.
